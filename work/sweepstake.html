Case study — In progress

Sweepstake

Turning one group's pool into software any club can run under its own name.

Role Product, architecture, build
Stack Python, Flask, SQLite, server-rendered
Status In build — pilot planned
Source Private · available on request

The idea

Rugby clubs, tennis clubs and offices all run a sweepstake for the one big event of their year, and almost all of them run it on a printed sheet or a group chat. One More Year proved the shape works for one group. This is the version a club buys once, brands with its own name and colours, and runs itself.

The hard part was never the draw. It was making an organiser confident about what they had just set up.

The interesting problem

A sweepstake is four decisions — what people pay, what the organisation keeps, what the prizes are, and where the prizes come from — and those four decisions combine into very different things. Free to enter with a trophy at the end is a raffle at a school fair. Everyone pays in and the winner takes the pot is something else entirely, with rules attached.

A club treasurer should not have to work that out from a form. So the app works it out, and after setup it says so in one sentence, in plain English, before anything is opened to members: 

def classify(entry_amount_cents, prizes):
    """Two axes decide what a pool actually is: is there a stake,
    and is any prize worth money. Everything else is detail."""
    stake = entry_amount_cents > 0
    valuable = any(p["kind"] in VALUABLE_KINDS for p in prizes)

    if stake and valuable:      return STAKE_AND_PRIZE
    if stake and not valuable:  return STAKE_NO_PRIZE
    if valuable:                return FREE_WITH_PRIZE
    return JUST_A_GAME

Excerpt — app/shape.py

That verdict sits at the top of the review page with the money broken down line by line: what comes in, what the named organisation keeps, what each place pays. An organiser who can see it is an organiser who will not get it wrong. There is a guardrail in the schema too — a pool can name an organisation as the beneficiary of its share, and cannot pay that share to a person.

Decision — deal the draw like a pack of cards

With twenty members and eight players in the field, everyone must get someone and the extras must be spread evenly. Picking at random per person gets you duplicates and a lonely eighth player. Shuffling once and dealing round-robin gets you [3,3,3,3,2,2,2,2] every time, and it is also the version you can explain to a room: 

options = shuffled(options)          # the field, once
people  = shuffled(paid_participants)  # unpaid are not in the draw

for i, person in enumerate(people):
    allocate(person, options[i % len(options)])

The shape of it — app/db.py

Decision — multi-tenant from the first table

Every row hangs off an org_id, and every query is scoped by it. Retrofitting tenancy onto a single-tenant schema is a rewrite; putting it in on day one costs one column. The organisation's colour is a single custom property on <body>, so a tenant's brand flows through the entire stylesheet without a build step or a second theme file.

Members never sign in. They open a link, type a name, and that is the whole flow — the same lesson as the last project, applied on purpose this time, with unguessable tokens rather than row ids from the start.

Where it is now

Schema, draw, organiser setup and the join flow are built. Still to come: the member view, results and standings, and a printable QR poster an organiser can pin to the clubroom wall. The pilot is a darts tournament at the end of the year, with a written enquiry to the regulator going in first — I would rather ask before I build the pricing than after.

What this one is teaching me

  • The product is the constraint, not the feature list. Cutting to one draw shape and one event took the build from months to weeks.
  • Say what the software just did. The summary screen is the thing a buyer would actually pay for.
  • Ask the boring questions early. Rules you find out about after launch are the expensive kind.
